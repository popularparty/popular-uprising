Chapter Four

Ascend Through the Spiral

¶What we have today is a society of permanent adolescents. Without the opportunity for generative risk and consequence, no one is allowed to grow up. There were never any “adults in the room.” The Regime has produced a population incapable of replacing it. In doing so, it has produced a population also incapable of sustaining it. The future is sacrificed to protect the past. The Regime is Saturn devouring his children.

¶It can be remarked then with some irony how young our civilization is. The constitutional settlement is less than two and a half centuries old. Its appearance of age despite this is proof against the Regime’s long-term viability. Rome governed itself, in continuous institutional descent, for over two thousand years. From the Republic, to the Principate, to the Eastern Empire. The architecture held and adapted and preserved continuity through massive transformations for an unfathomably long time.

¶For three thousand years, the West produced its governing class through ordeal. The Roman aristocrat earned his position through military command, public office, and the visible expenditure of his own fortune in service of Rome. The medieval knight earned his place through physical courage, where failure meant death. The merchant class earned their station through capital investment risked, with no guarantee of return. The cleric sacrificed through renunciation. Each estate had its own ordeal. To climb the social ladder was to risk something.

¶The estates system was not invented in any one place. It emerged independently across medieval Europe: the three estates in France, the Cortes in Spain, the Reichstag in the Empire, Parliament in England, the Sejm in Poland. Different content, different cultures, same structural function. The convergence is not coincidental. It is evidence that functional representation by role and stake is discovered organizational technology: the answer that complex societies arrive at naturally when they are solving the real problem of how to represent different kinds of personal stake. It survived many centuries, much longer than the Regime has, and was only put away after hereditary succession had sufficiently degenerated the ruling class.

¶The Catholic Church is the longest continuously operating institution in Western history. Arguably, world history. It has outlasted every government it has ever coexisted with, every empire that has attempted to capture it, and every heresy that has attempted to replace it. It has institutional memory measured in millennia. It selects, forms, and reproduces its own governing class through defined ordeals, vows, formation, renunciation. It elects its sovereign through a college whose members are themselves the product of the institution. This is the gold standard of self-perpetuating governance.

¶These institutions survived stresses the Regime has never been tested against. Every crisis leaves the Regime more brittle, its flawed institutional logic stretched even further. It has been coasting, riding the inertia of the most significant technological revolution in human history, on what was effectively a continent-sized gold mine. America succeeded despite her government. The men who built America were ambitious, productive, dangerous men who would have been suffocated to death by any sufficiently mature state, as their descendants are today. The founders’ genius being their failure to build such a state. There was always more land westward. There was always more capital to be made. There was always somewhere to flee from the institution that, given another century of consolidation, would have caught up with them. That escape valve closed roughly a century ago. The frontier ended and the continent filled: the resources no longer effectively infinite. As American exceptionalism has expired, the institution it has been disguising began to reveal itself. What we are watching unfold in real time is not a sudden collapse. It is the Regime now finally making contact with the country that had spent two centuries outrunning it.

¶While America has been coasting on inheritance, another civilization has been performing the operation we now require. The Chinese Communist Party did not import Western liberal architecture. They modernized their own civilization’s native organizational logic. The Mandarin system, their two thousand years of self-reproducing governing class formed through defined ordeal, was updated. The classic Confucian canon became the Marxist-Leninist-Maoist canon. The architecture persisted because it was solving a real civilizational problem and the Chinese understood that their answer to that problem was older and better tested than anything the Enlightenment had to offer them.

¶The result is one of the most dramatic state building successes in history. Continuous institutional coherence for three quarters of a century. One and a half billion people governed without the periodic constitutional crises that punctuate American political life. Meanwhile our political class is incapable of executing the most basic functions of government. Their state has coherence, ours is only united in its disunity.

¶This is not a recommendation that we copy them. Their tradition is alien to ours and they have their own problems of which there are many. The CCP’s ideological rigidity, information suppression, and its cycle of corruption and purges are all expressions of a single structural deficiency: the system has no legitimate channel through which reality can talk back to the governing class. It has poor epistemic hygiene. Doctrine doesn’t get revised, information doesn’t flow upward honestly, corruption doesn’t get addressed transparently. These are problems that Functional Representation already solves: representation by role and stake, those who pay the price decide. Functional Representation isn’t just a fairer system, it’s a superior informational processing system. Real information is surfaced about real conditions because representatives have direct material stake in what they represent. The CCP and the Regime have parallel failure modes. Both govern through abstractions that cannot be falsified by reality because reality lacks accurate representation in their feedback loops. But what the Chinese have proven is that a civilization’s native organizational logic can be properly modernized.

¶Finance is the technology by which capital is allocated across time and risk. A society that produces surplus must decide what to do with that surplus. It can be consumed, stored, or reinvested to produce more surplus. Finance is the apparatus that makes these decisions at scale. None of this is parasitic in itself. Without finance, a complex society cannot exist.

¶The purpose of finance is as a coordinating function, not a productive one. It allocates capital to those who do create value. When it allocates well, it amplifies the productive order. When it allocates poorly, it starves the productive order. The Regime inverted this relation. The coordinating apparatus has captured the productive order it was supposed to serve. The tail wags the dog. The men who allocate capital live outside the conditions their allocations create. Speculation has displaced investment. Asset prices have detached from underlying value. Bailouts socialize loss while privatizing gain. Finance under Functional Representation exists to serve the productive order. Financial decisions that destroy productive enterprise should destroy the financial actors who make them. Without consequence, allocation does not improve. Without improvement, finance isn’t performing its function, it’s performing extraction.

¶The Regime accidentally built a technology for economic coordination more powerful than pure market economies or pure planned economies, and it cannot use it to its full extent because the Regime is by nature uncoordinated. This technology gets wasted on protecting the rent-seeking monopolies of oligarchs.

¶Regulation, as it currently exists, is a system that shapes economic activity at every level: production standards, capital flows, market access, labor terms, environmental requirements, professional licensing. Both the libertarian right and the socialist left fundamentally misdescribe today’s economy because both are fighting yesterday’s ideological war. The libertarian sees regulation as the corruption of an underlying market, the socialist sees the market as the corruption of an underlying plan. Both miss that there is a third thing here that uses market mechanisms for information and incentive but operates within centrally engineered constraints. The Regime built this, but the Regime doesn’t understand the power of what it built.

¶The tragedy is that this technology has no director to wield it. Regulation under the Regime is captured by the regulated industries themselves, written by lobbyists for the firms that will be subject to it, used to throttle competition rather than to coordinate production, optimized for financial extraction rather than productive output just like everything else. The most sophisticated coordination technology ever built and it runs on disjointed fragments captured by petty factional interests. It’s a commandless command economy. Another face of the system that decides without a decider, plans without a planner, and coordinates without coordination.

¶Our position is that we treat regulation as what it actually is: game design. The economy is just a game and the regulations are the rules for it. Players will always try to min-max the most reward out of the current game environment. Set the rules poorly and you get degenerate play where everyone defects. Set them well and the players are optimized to choose behaviors that benefit the good of the public. The Regime built a corruption loop. Today’s rules get written by yesterday’s winners. Last year’s regulatory capturers select next year’s regulators. Last year’s tax beneficiaries fund the campaigns of next year’s tax writers. Bailout recipients lobby for more bailouts. This process has been recursive for generations and the whole process has become completely inbred. The rules are optimized to reward those who have already won and starve any competition that might replace them. The only move to break the loop is to separate winning from authorship, and bring the regulatory architecture under the design and direction of a Will that cannot be bought.

¶Will, unlike consensus, is not a coercive preference aggregation loop.

¶Will is a vector. It’s the act of choosing among possibilities. Of all the things that the Regime lacks, this is the most significant. The lack of Will makes course correction very very expensive. The new state will have a sovereign leader. But a sovereign leader need not be a dictator. He can’t be. That mistake has been made enough times. But it is a false binary that the choices are either no sovereign or tyranny. The Pope is a sovereign. He is elected, he is accountable to his polity, and he follows 2000 years of continued lawful succession. This is the institutional tradition to follow, not the autocrat, not the dictator.

¶The greatest single failure point for sovereign executives, and one of the most commonly recurring ones, is micromanagement. That is, making decisions on the operational level. The Sovereign exists to have taste and to delegate. He sets the direction for the polity on its most abstract level. He exists to decide when someone has to. When the daily function of the state is impeded, he makes the exception, he clears the way. That a sovereign ought to be even allowed to micromanage the state is lunacy. He is the ideas guy of the realm.

¶The sovereign’s job, beyond having taste and delegating, is to ensure coherence and success of the polity’s trajectory on a historical timeframe. To oversee the transition from past, to present, to future. To design, to authorize decisions whose consequences extend far beyond any electoral cycle or single lifetime. The cathedral, intercontinental infrastructure projects, long-horizon projects of historical importance. Will operates, and can be passed from successor to successor, on time horizons that consensus cannot reach.

¶Since the architecture constraining the sovereign office of the new state largely prevents him from over-deciding, the main failure point of dictators, kings, and other autocratic rulers, this failure point inevitably gets passed down to subordinates. It is they, now, who are making operational-level decisions. Their micro-tyranny is prevented, structurally, by being easily appointed and removed, being subject to judgement from above and not just below. They are retainers as much as they are executives.

¶There ought to be a graceful, honorable retirement for officers who underperform. Punishment for regular failure produces a culture in which failure is hidden and accumulates. Punishment should be reserved for corruption, treason, and betrayal.

¶The Church has understood for thousands of years that men with property cannot be incorruptible because property generates interests independent of the order they serve. The Regime’s political class has the property problem grossly unsolved. American politicians enter middle-class and leave rich, sometimes filthy rich. It is the alignment of the political career with the financial interests of those who fund it. The political class is not just corrupt, it is structurally compromised by the property relation itself. They own the same assets that benefit from the policies they pass. They retire into the firms of the interests they served. Their children are credentialed into the same class. The loop is recursive across generations and the political class becomes a hereditary aristocracy dressed in the garb of democracy.

¶The only full solution to this problem is a defined political class that is banned from owning property beyond the personal. No bank account. No land. Nothing. There can be no half measures. This is their renunciation. They will be like monks who subsist off the Church’s property. If a political class that subsists entirely off of public works wants to enrich itself, the only legal way to do so will be to create better, more beautiful public works. Everyone wins. Corruption for a public servant is a travesty. Ownership of money or property beyond the personal will be punished by indefinite expulsion and imprisonment. For the public servant the tie of property itself inherently creates a conflict of interest between the public good and his own interest. “Ye cannot serve God and mammon.”

¶America already functions as a one-party state in everything but name. The pretense of party competition is the cover under which the unitary system operates. Because the duopoly is dishonest about what it is, it cannot be designed for the public good. Design requires intention. Intention requires acknowledgement of what the thing even is. A one-party state that pretends to be a multi-party democracy can only operate by accident, and its accidents are optimized for the parasitic class because the parasitic class is the only constituency organized and powerful enough to take advantage of and direct the accidents. The duopoly’s incoherence is the immutable structural consequence of refusing to admit what the system is.

¶We propose a constitutionally integrated political class, organized as a single institution: the Popular Party, with defined membership, defined formation, defined renunciations, defined succession. The party is not a private association competing for office under the constitution. The Popular Party is a constitutional organ, part of the designed architecture itself. There is no political class outside the Popular Party. There are no second masters. There is no auction of nominations. There is nowhere to defect to. The political class will be visible, legible, and responsible to the public. The order is one. The vocation is one. The only rewards there can be for a public servant are increased responsibility, and the honor and glory in serving the people well.

¶The Regime will try to brand us authoritarians. It will be the first word the hypocrites reach for. The charge presumes that what we have now is freedom, that the duopoly is liberty, that unaccountable agencies is liberty, that the political class enriching itself is liberty, that the citizen reduced to a signature on decisions made elsewhere is liberty.

¶The Sovereign of our design is structurally prevented from making the operational decisions that define every actual authoritarianism. The Regime’s executive rules with no such constraint. Congress passes a vague enabling statute, and an unelected agency fills in the substance. The EPA alone effectively writes as much law in a year as Congress does in a decade. Functional Representation will make the new government answerable to the people in a way the federal system hasn’t for as long as anyone has been alive. Our political class will be bound to serve the public interest, barred from owning property entirely, while the Regime’s political class is defined by its inherent conflicts of interest. We have solved the corruption problem the Regime can’t even name. We will provide a succession that is lawful and continuous, where the Regime’s rotation of staff is a four-year convulsion of a state at war with itself.

¶The Regime’s thinking is that the only thing stopping authoritarianism is procedure. This is a fallacy. This logic gave us the Cult of Consensus theology and the hidden privatization of the state into an authoritarian plutocracy. The constraint that prevents tyranny is ontological. A sovereign who derives authority from the republic and converts the republic into his own personal property destroys the source of his authority. The Regime’s unnamed sovereign has quietly done this and gradually forsaken the trust of the people it depends on to govern. The Roman emperors who understood this, who maintained the forms and functions of the republic, generally governed well for decades and died quietly in their beds. The emperors who forgot this, who began treating the state as their own personal property, were typically killed by their own praetorian guard. Not because the guard had removal authority, but because the emperor had destroyed his own legitimacy. The only source left of his power was force, and force gets answered with force.

¶The evidence of this in our own time is January 6th. Trump had the mob, the moment, and enough institutional confusion that a more determined man might’ve pushed through. He did not, though there was no procedural mechanism to physically stop him. The Constitution, the vice president, the police. None of them stopped him. The procedure only named the line. What stopped him was the implicit understanding that crossing that line would’ve forfeited his constitutionally sanctified legitimacy. Trump would become a usurper.

¶The usurper, the dictator, who has made a new order entirely dependent on his personal force is maximally endangered. The entire system depends on him, and its opponents have a single high-value target: kill him and everything stops. Caesar is the archetypal story. Yet if an attempt were made against Augustus, the system would go on, and the assassins would be immediately subject to the wrath of whatever faction backed the succession. The Sovereign who understands this has a direct personal interest in building truly functional institutions beneath him rather than dependencies. Every institution that can operate without him is armor. Maintaining and advancing the interests of the institutions that give him legitimacy prevents him from relying on crude force.

¶The Pope holds total authority within his domain. He is elected, accountable to that institution, sovereign, and constrained by the same ontological logic: the Church does not belong to him. It belongs to Christ and the faithful, His body. To convert it into the Pope’s property would be to forfeit the thing that gives him power in the first place. The Church has had some bad leaders over its 2000 years. It has had crises. It has survived every single one of them without collapsing or being turned into a kind of dictatorship. It has survived for an unfathomably long time.

¶Likewise, our new state will not belong to the Sovereign, it will belong to the people. The Sovereign holds it in trust, deriving his authority from a source he cannot possess and cannot destroy without destroying himself. This is far more republican than what presently exists. The Regime’s hidden sovereign owns the state in fact while denying ownership in name. Our Sovereign holds the state in trust, and acknowledges that trust in name.

¶The Regime has betrayed the liberal project. It has privatized what was supposed to be for the public. There is no faction within the Regime with any intention of restoring what belongs to the people. We are the only liberals left. The Regime calls itself liberal when it is not. It has abandoned liberty in everything but diction. It is a despotism of delay, denial, and deflection. We are returning liberalism to its first principles: power made visible, authority made lawful, office made accountable, and government once again bound to the will of the governed.

¶The administrative design philosophy of the Popular Party is a spiral. The same principles ascend through every altitude: visible authority, functional representation, renunciation of the political class, decision authority running downward and information running upward. Each altitude performs its own work that no other altitude can perform. The school board is not a small Sovereign. The central government is not a large school board. They are different stations on a single ascending architecture, each doing only what it can do, all governed by the same operating principles.

¶The prefecture model has proven to work and it should be a reference point when designing the new administrative architecture. Japan has operated under it since the Meiji Restoration. France has operated some version of it since Napoleon. Neither has produced the same kind of administrative dysfunction the American federal system has produced over comparable periods. The model is not novel and it is not theoretical. It is demonstrated, durable, and well understood.

¶The difference from the American federal system is that the prefecture is an administrative subdivision of a unified state, not a competing decision maker. Each prefecture has a governor and an assembly. There is no question of which level holds final authority because the question is not architecturally available. Different prefectures administer the same national policy adapted to different local conditions and the state remains coherent.

¶The prefecture model is a strong starting point but it is not designed with the demands the Popular Party will make on the architecture. It scales across territory within a single nation. It does not pair the territorial dimension with a parallel architecture for representing function. It is not designed for adaptability across time and scale at the rate adaptation now requires.

¶What we propose is a unitary state divided vertically into tiered territorial administrative levels and divided horizontally where major social functions report upward through their own organs. In practice, this means that every layer of government would have three organs, an executive administrator, a territorial governing body reporting local territorial conditions, and a functional assembly reporting the conditions of social functions. The prefect executes national direction downward, the assemblies carry organized knowledge upward. The state will see both the map and the machinery of society.

¶The territorial channel and the functional channel report on overlapping conditions through entirely different chains of accountability. Vertically, the borough reports to the city, which reports to the prefecture, which reports to the province, which reports to the national district, which reports to the center. Horizontally, there is a separate structure for each major function: agriculture, energy, industry, transport, education, policing, finance, health, science, culture, etc. At every altitude where both channels operate, the information can be cross-checked at the point of overlap. The school board has a territorial position (the local district) and a functional position (education as a function). At each step up the ladder, the territorial reporting and the functional reporting can be checked against each other. The executive authority at any altitude has multiple independent reports on conditions within its scope that can be triangulated and discrepancies investigated.

¶At the level of the borough and the city, direct election is operationally meaningful in a way that it ceases to be at higher altitudes. The polity is small enough that residents can plausibly know the candidates personally, evaluate them on direct evidence, and be governed by them in ways that correspond to local conditions the residents themselves experience. There is also a vocational reason for preserving local elections. The political class needs to be recruited from somewhere, and local political experience is a natural proving ground. A city councilor who demonstrates competence and is recognized by the local apparatus as suitable for promotion within the political class can ascend through the spiral. Local elections function partly as a talent identification mechanism.

¶However, local administration as the recruitment ground for the political class inherently produces a brain drain at the local level that must be corrected. The competent get promoted upward through the spiral and the local apparatus is left with whoever did not advance. Every existing system that uses local government as a training ground suffers from it, including the American federal system.

¶Our proposed solution to this problem has two components. Firstly, make an age ceiling on entry into the political class to prevent the seniority pipeline from getting clogged. Secondly, make a retirement age for high office, but no retirement age for local offices. This prevents gerontocracy at the top, and also brings talent back to the local level. Local government gets to be both a proving ground for young men of the political class, and a place for the elderly experienced statesmen to retire to and transmit their knowledge. This doesn’t just preserve talent at every institutional level, but creates a natural continuity of institutional memory. With this problem solved, the question of selection at each altitude can be specified.

¶The functional estates elect their representatives from within at every altitude. The information flowing upward through the functional channel is filtered through the estate’s own competency judgments. Those voting to represent their functions will always have a direct stake in the elections, and have at least a baseline level of experience to judge their representatives. This isn’t the case for the territorial dimension.

¶Above the local levels, the borough and the city, general territorial elections cease to be meaningful mechanisms for achieving representation. The prefect, the regional administrator, the national administrator, these positions are appointed from above through the chain that runs from the Sovereign down. This is entirely structurally necessary. An elected prefect would have a constituency distinct from the central government’s and would be structurally tempted towards repeating the mistakes of the federal system through other means. The polity at this level is too large, too diverse, too strategically complex, and too distant from ordinary public life for any electorate to meaningfully evaluate candidates. We would return to the same problem of the officeholder representing people with “nothing in common but a zip code.” We would run into the same problem of preference aggregation. The representative’s interests would be necessarily captured. Representation would drift to whoever can move the aggregate, not to the polity that the aggregate purports to represent.

¶The body that elects the sovereign is constituted by a body of the party’s most elite officers entering the prime of their career. Structurally similar to the College of Cardinals, they elect a member from within. The papal model succeeds at continuity, legitimacy, and elite selection, but fails on sclerosis, gerontocracy, informational decay, poor integration between elector and governing brain, and suboptimal distribution of experience across the whole of the organization. Our model for sovereign selection corrects the papal model at every known failure point.

¶Our college should be composed of men under the age of 40. This is a role for younger men in their intellectual prime who have proven peak aptitude. This college is not primarily an electoral body. The election of the Sovereign is a rare event within the college’s other work. Its function is the legal and intellectual general staff of the sovereign’s office: to be involved in all branches of government, executive, legislative, and judiciary, and across all domains. The college is the hand of the Sovereign. The college’s members might rotate through different roles during their decade or so of service, investigation, prosecution, administration, legal drafting and review, etc. The sovereign serves long enough to have strategic perspective, with the surrounding institutional brain refreshing rapidly enough to prevent sclerosis.

¶As this body is responsible for electing the next sovereign from within itself, there is a direct cultural, intellectual, and strategic continuity from one Sovereign to the next. Despite their youth, the members of the college are uniquely qualified for selecting the next Sovereign from within their own ranks, due to observing each other performing the demanding work across all areas of government, and personally working alongside the Sovereign himself. They are evaluating potential candidates for the apex position from the position just below the apex. Their decision is therefore better informed than any alternative.

¶The Sovereign is elected from among the college’s members during his own college tenure. He assumes office in his late twenties or thirties, at the moment of cognitive prime, with potentially three or four decades of governance ahead of him. Most college members may never participate in an election. They will spend their decade or so in the college doing the college’s other work and then exit to other roles within the government for the prime of their career proper, carrying with them the experience and intellectual formation built during their college service. The party career must be designed so that there is no terminal endpoint where all experience and talent gets sucked into one organ of government.

¶One potential blueprint of the new state is as follows. One sovereign office, whose officeholder carries total responsibility for the state’s decisions. His job is closer to a supreme legislator than a supreme executive. The Sovereign’s job isn’t to manage the day-to-day operations of the state like a normal head of state. He designs the rules of the game from outside of it. If the rulemaker is beholden to yesterday’s rules, then yesterday’s rules are the author of tomorrow's rules, with the rulemaker only as an instrument of yesterday’s rules. The Sovereign needs to not have factional, career, or political interests that shape his decision making. He is not supposed to be a political player. Those who actually play the game, that is, those who do the governing during normal operations, are playing within the rules that he creates. Beneath the Sovereign, a familiar separation of powers.

¶There is a Chief Secretary, the chief administration officer who assumes the administrative responsibilities similar to those of the President under the present U.S. federal system. The House of Representatives is replaced by a House of Functional Representatives. Senators are elected by party members. The judiciary should be split into two bodies: an advisory council of elders that also handles constitutional review, and a high court for appellate jurisdiction.

¶A good Sovereign ought to let the government function on its own during ordinary conditions. He’s structurally incentivized against micromanaging. Micromanagement creates room for making personal failures in judgment with almost zero potential upside. Though he delegates responsibility, the ultimate decision authority should rest clearly and unambiguously with him. The legislature’s passage of a bill is not the completion of a legislative act. It’s a recommendation backed by the information of those who know. The Sovereign’s assent is what makes it law. Any law or constitutional amendment should be required, except in the case of emergency, to pass through the legislature and the council and be reviewed as a formality before being signed into law by the Sovereign. There will always be a sovereign body within any state, and it is in the best interest of the democratic process that it be as publicly legible and accountable as possible.

¶The legislature still writes and debates the laws, the executive still administers the laws, the judiciary still rules on the laws, but the people know that every decision is from the Sovereign: that he has ultimate decision-making responsibility.

¶To be judged well by history, we must do more than take power and build institutions. We must build the men and women who will inhabit and embody those institutions.

¶The new order cannot recruit from a population trained for compliance. The functional estates cannot represent vocations the schools have refused to honor. Everything proposed in this document depends on raising children differently than the Regime raises them.

¶The Regime's school works tolerably for the student who fits its mold: textually intelligent, sequentially patient, above all compliant. For every other student it does almost nothing but issue, at the end, a certificate of completion whose value declines with every passing year. The system mistakes equal dignity for identical treatment, and identical treatment produces unequal failure. The academically gifted are slowed down. The practically gifted are bored. The physically courageous are domesticated. “No Child Left Behind” leaves children behind because it refuses to ask what kind of formation each child uniquely requires.

¶Our principle is singular: education must be differentiated rather than uniform. Formation means working with the material you have. A common foundation is owed to every child. Beyond it, the path forks: academic, technical, vocational, martial, civic, each track with its own gate, its own path, its own dignity. By the middle teenage years, students should be encountering real choices, real standards, and real consequences under supervision. We will stop preventing young people from becoming adults.

¶Higher learning must be returned from the terminal point of a credentialing hierarchy to the intellectually serious. The university is not supposed to be a four-year summer camp for the developmentally arrested. It should be for those dedicated to higher study, whatever path brought them there.

¶The Regime has raised a compliant mass of credentialed consumers. The successor order must raise functional adults: those who will bear the weight of civilization as it grows heavier and carry it forward. In the age that lies ahead, this work becomes a world-historical task.
